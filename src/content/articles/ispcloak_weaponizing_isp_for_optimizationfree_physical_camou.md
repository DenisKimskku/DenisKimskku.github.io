# ISPCloak: Exploiting Invertible Image Signal Processing to Evade Deepfake Detectors via RAW-Domain Physical Camouflage

As generative models become capable of synthesizing photorealistic media, the task of verifying image authenticity falls onto automated deepfake detectors. Automated trust-and-safety pipelines deployed by web platforms and verification APIs typically seek out mathematical irregularities or high-frequency digital artifacts left behind by generative networks. However, a systemic blind spot in these systems has been exposed: existing detectors operate entirely within a digital paradigm, ignoring the fundamental physical and hardware-intrinsic characteristics of real-world image acquisition. 

By operating purely on RGB pixel distributions, current detectors are highly vulnerable to adversarial perturbations that accurately emulate physical sensor signatures. The newly proposed ISPCloak framework weaponizes this vulnerability, showing that when synthetic images are wrapped in realistic physical noise, leading deepfake detectors fail to flag them.

### Background and Problem Setup

In actual cameras, when photons strike a Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) sensor, they generate Poisson-distributed shot noise proportional to the signal intensity ($\sigma^2 \propto \mu$). Simultaneously, read noise is introduced by thermal and electronic fluctuations. When these RAW sensor readings pass through a camera's hardware-intrinsic ISP pipeline (encompassing demosaicing, white balance, tone mapping, and denoising), these independent noise patterns are transformed into complex spatial and chromatic correlations.

AI generators, by contrast, operate in RGB pixel or compressed latent spaces and default to a physics-violating, constant noise variance profile ($\sigma^2 \approx \text{const}$). Forensic detectors exploit this clean, noise-consistent digital gap. 

Prior evasion attempts rely on optimization-heavy, iterative gradient-descent approaches that are computationally expensive and easily overfit to specific surrogate models. The table below compares the positioning of ISPCloak against these alternative paradigms:

### Methodology

The ISPCloak pipeline operates as a continuous, feedforward framework designed to suppress generative digital artifacts and seamlessly inject authentic physical sensor noise.

```
+------------------+     +-----------------------+     +-------------------+
|  AI-Generated    | --> |  Generative Artifact  | --> |   Invertible ISP  |
|    RGB Image     |     |   Suppression (DnCNN) |     |  (InvISP Network) |
+------------------+     +-----------------------+     +-------------------+
                                                                 |
                                                                 v
+------------------+     +-----------------------+     +-------------------+
| Adversarial RGB  | <-- |      Forward ISP      | <-- | RAW-Domain Noise  |
|    Output (x*)   |     |    Reconstruction     |     |  Injection & Clip |
+------------------+     +-----------------------+     +-------------------+
```

#### Step 1: Generative Artifact Suppression
To prevent synthetic patterns from clashing with the injected camera noise, ISPCloak first purifies the input image $x$. It uses a pre-trained Denoising Convolutional Neural Network (DnCNN) [49] to predict the high-frequency generative residual $R$:

$$R = \mathcal{F}(x)$$

To preserve visual quality and prevent blurring in flat areas, an adaptive gradient magnitude mask $M$ is constructed:

$$G = \sqrt{(\nabla_h x)^2 + (\nabla_v x)^2 + \eta}$$

$$M = \left( \frac{G}{\max(G)} \right)^\gamma$$

where $\nabla_h$ and $\nabla_v$ represent spatial gradient operators, $\eta$ is a stabilization constant, and $\gamma$ is a hyperparameter set to a default value of $2.500$. The purified image $x'$ is computed as:

$$\tilde{R} = M \odot R$$

$$x' = \text{Clip}(x - \alpha \tilde{R}, 0, 1)$$

where $\alpha$ is a blend weight set to $0.200$.

#### Step 2: Inverse ISP Mapping (RGB to RAW)
Using a pre-trained Invertible Image Signal Processing (InvISP) network [46], the purified RGB image is mapped back into its single-channel Bayer-mosaiced RAW representation:

$$r = \Phi^{-1}(x')$$

#### Step 3: Physical Sensor Noise Injection
Within the RAW photonic domain, ISPCloak injects physically accurate shot (Poisson) and read (Gaussian) noise:

$$r' = r + n_p + n_g$$

$$n_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_p^2 |r|), \quad n_g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_g^2 I)$$

The scaling coefficients are set to defaults of $\sigma_p = 0.090$ and $\sigma_g = 0.075$. To maintain strict visual imperceptibility, the perturbed RAW values are constrained within a narrow bound $\epsilon = 0.006$ of the original RAW values:

$$\hat{r} = \text{Clip}(r', r - \epsilon, r + \epsilon)$$

#### Step 4: Forward ISP Reconstruction
Finally, the noisy RAW data is projected back to RGB space using the forward ISP model:

$$x^* = \Phi(\hat{r})$$

This forward pass naturally distributes the RAW noise across channels and spatial neighbors, generating authentic demosaicing and color interpolation footprints.

### Limitations & Open Questions

While ISPCloak achieves high transferability, security practitioners must analyze its real-world constraints before declaring deepfake detection fully broken:

1. **The LGrad Exception**: As seen in the results, LGrad (which operates in a gradient-map space) remains the most resilient baseline detector, holding ISPCloak's ASR to 59.10% on GenImage and 54.86% on WildFake. This indicates that gradient-domain analysis is partially robust against RAW-domain noise.
2. **Channel Stability and Compression**: Standard media upload pipelines compress images heavily and downscale resolutions. High-frequency RAW-domain sensor noise may be stripped out during this process, potentially rendering the attack less effective in real-world transit.
3. **ISP Calibration Dependency**: The InvISP network must be trained on authentic camera sensor distributions. If an attacker uses an InvISP calibrated on a specific camera model to cloak an image, but the victim pipeline looks for a broader or different sensor signature, the evasion rate may degrade.

---

### What Practitioners Should Do

Practitioners must update their forensic pipelines now to defend against RAW-domain physical camouflage:

1. **Incorporate Compression-Resilience Training**: Since physical noise attacks rely on high-frequency, sub-pixel structures, train target detectors with aggressive compression augmentations to force models to focus on structural artifacts rather than noise texture.
2. **Deploy Multi-Scale Gradient Detectors**: Implement gradient-space feature extraction methods like LGrad [36] rather than relying solely on spatial-domain CNNs or frequency-domain transformers, as gradient representations demonstrate the highest empirical resistance to physical noise injection.
3. **Simulate Physical-to-Digital Processing in Defense Loops**: Standardize on dual-domain defense checks. During inference, pass suspected images through a software-emulated denoising filter (using artifact-removal networks such as DnCNN [49]) to strip high-frequency noise before feeding the image to the classifier. If a classifier's decision flips drastically from "Real" to "Fake" after denoising, flag the image for manual review.

### The Takeaway

ISPCloak demonstrates that deepfake detectors are overfitted to the digital anomalies of AI generation, ignoring the physical realities of camera hardware. By moving the adversarial playfield from RGB pixels to RAW sensor modeling, the framework bypasses existing forensic detection mechanisms with minimal computational cost. Future media forensics must shift from purely data-driven classification toward physics-aware models that verify hardware-intrinsic footprints.

## Den's Take

For years, forensic researchers have built deepfake detectors on a house of cards, assuming that synthetic artifacts in RGB space are robust signatures of generation. This work demonstrates how easily that assumption crumbles when you shift the battleground to the raw sensor domain. By injecting signal-dependent Poisson-Gaussian noise and utilizing an invertible ISP pipeline, ISPCloak achieves a highly efficient bypass of detectors like SAFE, offering a 150x speedup over optimization-heavy baselines.

However, the evaluation reveals a critical gap that practitioners must not ignore. The authors' threat model assumes a standard, known forward ISP pipeline. In the real world, smartphone manufacturers deploy highly proprietary, closed-source neural ISPs that dynamically adjust parameters based on ambient light and scene semantic maps. I predict that against these custom, device-specific ISP pipelines, the transferability of a generic ISPCloak attack will drop significantly because the synthetic noise won't match the actual sensor signature.

As I have noted when reviewing other adversarial visual attacks against vision-language models, pixel-level forensic detectors are highly vulnerable to static input distribution anomalies. If platforms want to secure their automated image verification pipelines, they must stop looking solely for high-frequency artifacts and shift toward robust physical-domain modeling and hardware-level signature standards.
