# Tracking IoC Propagation via Watermarked Binary Traversal

## TLDR
*   **What**: Active measurement framework tracks IoC movement across the TI ecosystem.
*   **Who's at risk**: Security vendors and TI platforms relying on opaque sharing practices.
*   **Key number**: Only 17% of 30 vendors shared extracted TI.

## The Disconnect Between Extraction and Action
The threat intelligence (TI) market is growing rapidly, driven by the need for timely data on cyber threats. Defenders rely on a globally distributed ecosystem of security vendors and altruists to analyze malicious artifacts, such as binaries, and produce Indicators of Compromise (IoCs). While vendors perform analysis—often rapidly—a significant gap exists between generating intelligence and acting on it. Prior studies have looked at sandbox environments in isolation, focusing on static or behavioral fingerprints malware might use to evade detection. However, these isolated views fail to capture the global dynamics of IoC propagation or the actual sharing practices among vendors. We observe that while sandbox analysis is widespread (performed by 67% of vendors), the actual sharing of extracted TI is severely limited, indicating a structural weakness in how intelligence moves from detection to defense.

## Provenance Trail via Domain Encoding
The core insight of this work is using a unique, traceable mechanism to follow IoCs as they move between different ecosystem participants. Instead of passively waiting for intelligence to appear in public feeds, we actively probe the system. The key is embedding provenance information directly into the network artifacts generated by the malware. We design binaries, called Rockets, to emit unique domain names upon execution. These domain names are not random; they are structured to encode the specific sandbox environment fingerprint ($H_i$) and the execution ID ($\epsilon_i$). This structure allows the Observer to reconstruct the propagation chain, distinguishing between a binary execution and a simple IoC lookup, which was previously infeasible.

## The Rocket and Satellite Mechanism
The measurement pipeline involves a Generator and an Observer. The Generator creates a defanged binary, the Rocket, which has suspicious behavior designed to trigger malicious verdicts. When executed, the Rocket emits a unique domain name containing encoded information about the sandbox environment. It then generates and drops a modified copy, the Satellite, on the host. This Satellite is uniquely linked to the parent Rocket via appended IDs in its domain name, enabling tracking when another vendor executes it. The system's fingerprint relies on three selected features: system manufacturer, RAM, and OS install date. The Satellite domain name format is: `⟨b ∥ϵnHn|...|ϵ1H1⟩`, where $b$ is the original submission ID. The Observer uses Emission Sensors to log DNS and HTTP emissions from these domains. To measure timeliness, the Observer deploys an IoC Probe that checks various TI sources, such as Google Safe Browsing or VirusTotal, to see how quickly the IoCs are indexed or blocked.

## Limitations
The measurement framework relies on the successful execution and network emission of the deliberately crafted binaries. The threat models examined focus on vendor behavior and evasion tactics; the paper does not explore sophisticated, multi-stage adversary campaigns designed to mimic internal TI processes. Furthermore, the fidelity of the ecosystem reconstruction depends on the Observer successfully capturing all relevant network emissions, which could be hampered by advanced network obfuscation techniques not accounted for in the current design.

## What practitioners should do
*   Prioritize diversification of sandbox fingerprints to prevent adversaries from exploiting predictable environment signatures.
*   Implement provenance tracking mechanisms in internal threat analysis pipelines to map data lineage.
*   Establish monitoring for sharing delays between internal analysis stages and external blocklisting services.
*   Develop detection signatures against the known abuse patterns identified during the responsible disclosure process.

## Verdict
Read this paper if you are working on the operational security of threat intelligence pipelines or developing adversarial ML tools targeting TI infrastructure. Skip it if you are focused solely on malware static analysis without considering data flow.

## Den's Take

The focus on tracking IoC propagation via domain encoding is an interesting technical approach to observing ecosystem behavior, but the paper underestimates the complexity of real-world TI operations. Merely observing *where* an IoC lands doesn't tell you *why* it landed there or what the downstream impact is. The measurement framework, which relies on deliberately crafted binaries, is fundamentally limited to observing the *reporting* layer, not the *decision-making* layer. Vendors aren't just passive sensors; they have internal triage, prioritization, and confidence scoring mechanisms that filter what gets shared. The 17% sharing rate mentioned is a symptom of this internal friction, not just a failure of the transmission mechanism.
