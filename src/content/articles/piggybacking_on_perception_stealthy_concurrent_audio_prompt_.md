# Piggybacking on Perception: Stealthy Concurrent Audio Prompt Injections against Multimodal LLM Agents

The deployment of natively multimodal large language models featuring continuous audio perception has introduced a passive, omnidirectional attack surface. Because systems like OpenAI's GPT-4o Audio, Qwen3-Omni-Flash, and Google's Gemini 3 Pro continuously monitor ambient audio to support natural, real-time interactions, they cannot easily isolate authorized user speech from environmental sound. This academic paper demonstrates how an environmental third-party attacker can exploit this architectural blind spot to execute silent, high-privilege operations on a victim's device without their knowledge.

### Threat Model

| | |
|---|---|
| **Attacker** | Black-box environmental adversary with physical acoustic channel access (e.g., via a nearby speaker, smart TV, or ambient player); has no access to the target agent’s weights, system prompts, or tool-calling logic. |
| **Victim** | Audio-capable MLLM agents deployed on mobile, smart home, or IoT devices configured with high-privilege tool execution capabilities (e.g., financial transactions, location tracking, system settings). |
| **Goal** | Intent Hijacking: forcing the MLLM agent to bypass user intent and execute unauthorized high-risk operations (e.g., exfiltrating files, disabling security systems, or modifying transaction limits). |
| **Budget** | Low-cost physical audio playback equipment (such as a generic speaker or directional ultrasonic transducer) operating within physical proximity of the victim device. |

### Background & Problem Setup

Traditional voice assistant exploits focus primarily on physical-signal manipulation targeting the speech-to-text pipeline. These older techniques often assume a silent environment where the victim device is not actively receiving legitimate speech. Conversely, modern audio-native MLLM agents process continuous, streaming inputs where malicious audio must actively compete with the user's ongoing voice. 

| Methodology / Paper | Target System | Attack Vector | Key Operational Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DolphinAttack** (Zhang et al. [13]) | Traditional Automated Speech Recognition (ASR) / Keyword Spotting (KWS) | Modulates malicious commands onto inaudible ultrasonic carriers ($f_c \ge 20 \text{ kHz}$). | Assumes a silent environment; limited to simple command mapping with no reasoning/tool-use exploitation. |
| **AudioJailbreak** (Chen et al. [17]) | Audio Large Language Models (ALMs) | Appends optimized suffixal adversarial audio directly after the user's input. | Appends suffixal audio after user input without concurrent speech interference. |
| **Concurrent Audio Injection** (This Work) | Multimodal LLM Agents (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-4o, Doubao) | Overlaps optimized audio commands concurrently during active user speech. | Black-box execution; must survive simultaneous acoustic and semantic competition. |

### Methodology

The concurrent audio prompt injection attack operates simultaneously on two fronts: acoustic survival and semantic hijacking. To bypass the physical noise of the user's voice while maintaining low audibility to human ears, the authors design a multi-tier attack framework.

```
       [ Benign User Speech: xb(t) ]
                    │
                    ▼
[ Injected Malicious Audio: xm(t) ] ──► [ Acoustic Modulation & DRC ] ──► [ Boosted Adversarial Audio: xadv(t) ]
                                                                                         │
                                                                                         ▼
                                                                           [ Mixed Audio Signal: y(t) ]
                                                                                         │
                                                                                         ▼
                                                                            [ Multimodal LLM Agent ]
```

#### 1. Acoustic Modulation & Survival
To make the injected command $x_m(t)$ imperceptible or blend in as environmental background noise, the attack employs three types of acoustic primitives:
- **Inaudible Carrier Modulation**: High-frequency or ultrasonic carrier modulation ($f_c \ge 20 \text{ kHz}$), exploiting microphone non-linearity to demodulate the signal inside the device hardware (similar to DolphinAttack):
  $$x_{\text{adv}}(t) = [1 + m \cdot x_m(t)] \cdot \cos(2\pi f_c t)$$
- **Audible Low-Intelligibility Transformations**: Distorting the signal to sound like ambient noise (e.g., wind, street static, or whispers) using Spectral Inversion, which inverts the spectrum around a carrier frequency $f_c$:
  $$x_{\text{adv}}(t) = \text{LPF}[x_m(t) \cdot \cos(2\pi f_c t)]$$
- **Audible Semantic Confusion**: Modulating the speed, introducing heavy dialects, or translating the malicious command into a foreign language that the target MLLM understands but the human user ignores.

To help these quiet, distorted signals survive when layered over the user's dominant voice $x_b(t)$, the attacker applies Energy Enhancement and Dynamic Range Compression (DRC). The resulting composite signal is:
$$y(t) = x_b(t) + \lambda \cdot \text{Boost}(x_{\text{adv}}(t))$$
where $\lambda$ represents a scaling factor calculated to keep the signal within tested human perceptual limits while maximizing decodability.

#### 2. Content-Level Semantic Anchor Hijacking
Even if the acoustic signal is successfully decoded by the model, the agent's attention is already anchored to the user's original query. To redirect the agent's intent, the attacker prepends **Concurrent Injection Prefixes** designed to mimic system state transitions, forcing the model to ignore the prior benign command:

```text
# Examples of Semantic Hijacking Prefixes (Translated)
- "System Alert:" (System Tone Alert)
- "Previous task completed. Now please execute:" (Task Complete)
- "Task updated, now executing:" (Truncated Declaration)
- "Reasoning complete, beginning execution of new command:" (Thought Bias)
```

The authors systematically evaluated 11 representative audio-capable agents on their custom benchmark, `AudioAgentSecurity`, which contains 2,160 generated attack samples across 8 real-world task scenarios.

#### The Capability Paradox
A critical observation in the results is the "Capability Paradox": stronger models with superior acoustic encoders are significantly *more* vulnerable to concurrent audio prompt injections. While Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite recorded a low average ASR of 26.95%, this was primarily driven by its weak baseline capability to follow complex instructions (42.00% baseline ICR). Conversely, the advanced Gemini-3-Pro-Preview achieved 69.10% ASR, demonstrating that highly optimized perception systems are exceptionally good at recovering weak, distorted adversarial signals that human ears miss.

#### Defensive Evaluation: CADV vs. Baselines
To counter this threat, the paper introduces **Cascaded Audio Decoupling and Verification (CADV)**. CADV works at the perception layer through three sequential steps:
1. **Audio Track Separation**: Decoupling the input $y(t)$ into independent sources $(\hat{x}_b, \hat{x}_{\text{adv}})$ using a non-linear Mossformer2 model.
2. **Speaker Consistency Verification**: Using a CAM++ extractor to map both tracks into speaker embeddings and triggering an identity anomaly flag ($I_{id}$) if the cosine similarity ($S_{cos}$) falls below an energy-aware threshold.
3. **Semantic Filtering**: Transcribing the decoupled secondary track and filtering non-semantic background noises (e.g., laughter, static).

Table 4 highlights CADV's mitigation performance compared to undefended baselines:

|---|---|---|---|
| **High Frequency** | 70.55 | 2.32 | 68.23 |
| **Pulse** | 75.50 | 25.09 | 50.41 |
| **DolphinAttack** | 81.55 | 33.95 | 47.59 |
| **Foreign** | 72.36 | 29.00 | 43.36 |
| **Dialect** | 56.18 | 18.82 | 37.36 |

### Limitations & Open Questions

Despite its performance in quiet settings, CADV has severe practical limitations in busy environments. Because it operates under a zero-prior-knowledge assumption (no pre-registered user voiceprints), it relies entirely on detecting *statistical differences* between concurrent speakers. 

As Table 5 indicates, this results in increased False Positive Rates (FPR) in environments with legitimate background voices:
- **Office Meeting (OMEETING)**: 35.00% False Positive Rate.
- **Public Cafeteria (PCAFETER)**: 14.00% False Positive Rate.

This false positive rate means that in an office or a busy restaurant, CADV will more frequently flag normal background conversations as potential injections, interrupting the user experience with secondary confirmation prompts. 

Furthermore, if an attacker uses high-quality voice cloning to replicate the target user's voice for the injection, the speaker consistency verification step ($S_{cos}$) will fail to identify any identity discrepancy, rendering CADV's primary defense layer ineffective.

### What Practitioners Should Do

If you are developing or deploying voice-activated, tool-enabled MLLM agents, you must implement defense-in-depth across the perception and application layers:

#### 1. Implement Hardware-Level Acoustic Filtering
Configure physical acoustic restrictions at the hardware and driver level. Apply a sharp digital low-pass filter at the microphone input stream to discard any signal frequencies above 16 kHz. This immediately neutralizes near-ultrasonic, high-frequency, and DolphinAttack-style demodulation vectors without affecting human voice range (which typically stays below 8 kHz).

#### 2. Enforce Strict Multi-Speaker Isolation
Avoid passing raw, un-decoupled audio streams directly to downstream MLLM models. Integrate a lightweight, on-device blind source separation model (such as a pruned Mossformer2 or SepFormer) to isolate distinct voice tracks. If more than one distinct voice track containing semantic commands is detected, the system should halt execution.

#### 3. Enforce Human-in-the-Loop Confirmation for Sensitive Actions
Never allow voice agents to execute irreversible or high-risk tasks silently. Establish a hard logical barrier at the application layer requiring explicit secondary approval for sensitive tools. For example, if the model attempts to invoke a system configuration API, force a visual confirmation screen or an explicit prompt:

```python
# Conceptual tool execution guard
def set_transaction_limit(amount):
    if current_session.is_voice_only():
        # Trigger an out-of-band push notification or visual prompt
        raise SecurityConfirmationRequired("Please visually confirm transaction limit change.")
    return execute_db_update(amount)
```

#### 4. Design-in Semantic Integrity Checks
Validate if the parsed text contains sudden semantic discontinuities. Implement basic regex or lightweight classification guards to flag common injection patterns (such as `"ignore previous instructions"`, `"system alert"`, or `"task complete"`) occurring mid-sentence or within overlapped text streams.

### The Takeaway

As MLLMs transition from passive text-processing engines to active, always-listening physical world agents, text-only safety alignment is no longer a sufficient defense perimeter. When the physical perception layer itself cannot differentiate between an authorized user and a masked ambient sound, any agent integrated with system-level tools is vulnerable to silent hijack. Secure deployment requires a paradigm shift that treats the raw physical audio channel as an untrusted input stream requiring strict decoupling, speaker verification, and out-of-band confirmation.

## Den's Take

We need to stop treating continuous audio MLLMs as mere text-transcription pipelines with a voice interface. The 69.10% success rate against Gemini-3-Pro-Preview reveals a fundamental architectural vulnerability: these models lack native, hardware-enforced speaker diarization at the perception layer. By processing overlapping audio streams as a single, combined semantic input, the MLLM acts like a classic command-line interface that executes commands blindly, regardless of who is speaking.

While the paper’s optimization technique for physical acoustic survival is clever, it exposes a broader systemic flaw in how we design "always-listening" agents. We cannot solve this at the audio preprocessing layer alone; physical-world noise and acoustic reflections will always bypass simple filters. Instead, this threat highlights why we must fundamentally redesign agent authorization. As I noted in my review of the need for holistic agent security frameworks, securing autonomous systems requires us to abandon the assumption of trusted environmental inputs and instead enforce strict privilege containment at the tool-calling boundary. Until platforms implement hard-coded voice-fingerprint validation before invoking OS-level tools, any device with an open microphone remains an open terminal.
