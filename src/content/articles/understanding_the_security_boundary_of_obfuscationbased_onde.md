# Collapse Attack on Obfuscation-Based On-Device LLM Protection

## TLDR
*   **What**: A systematic attack, Collapse, exploits structural invariants in obfuscation primitives.
*   **Who's at risk**: On-device LLMs protected by TEE-Shielded LLM Partition (TSLP) methods.
*   **Key number**: Collapse can extract high-fidelity surrogate models from prior defenses.

## The Heuristic Nature of Prior TSLP Defenses
The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) on end devices aims to minimize latency and protect user data. Since these models are proprietary, their deployment on local hardware necessitates protection against model theft, especially when the execution is split between a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) and an untrusted Rich Execution Environment (REE). TEE-Shielded LLM Partition (TSLP) methods address this by offloading compute-intensive linear operators to the REE while keeping lightweight operations inside the TEE. To prevent the REE from stealing model weights ($\mathbf{W}$), these methods apply various obfuscation schemes—such as column-wise permutation, scaling, additive masking, or low-rank masking—before offloading the weights ($\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{W}} = O(\mathbf{W})$). While these schemes appear secure because they resist simple inversion attempts, they are largely heuristic. Each scheme invents tricks based on specific, ad-hoc algebraic operations. The paper formalizes a set of obfuscation primitives, defined as dual-tuples of linear computations satisfying specific algebraic properties. We demonstrate that the matrix-level weight transformations studied in several prominent TSLP methods published in top-tier venues can be expressed as compositions of these primitives. Concretely, we identify two categories of primitives, multiplicative primitives (permutation $\Pi$ and scaling $D$) and additive primitives (sparse mask $S$ and low-rank mask $L$). Each primitive is invertible and efficiently-computable, so any combination of them is also efficiently-computable within TEE’s secure enclave. Given these unified primitives, we identify $\text{Oprior}$ as a canonical structural form for the family generated by these primitives, which represents the security boundary of these methods.

## Oprior as the Structural Boundary
By establishing a common algebraic abstraction, the authors define the security boundary of this entire family as $\text{Oprior}$. Theorem 3 asserts that the canonical form of these primitive compositions, denoted as $\text{Oprior}$, characterizes the structural boundary of this primitive family. This means that regardless of how complex or layered a prior TSLP defense is, it can be reduced to this canonical structure.

## Collapse and the Failure of Invariants
Specifically, we develop a novel attack methodology Collapse against $\text{Oprior}$. Our key insight is that the column directions of the protected model weight $\mathbf{W}_{\text{vic}}$ cannot be effectively masked by arbitrary composition of these primitives. Specifically, column-wise permutation $\Pi$ and column-wise scaling $D$ only permute and rescale columns, so the column directions of $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$ are preserved and remain correlated with the public $\mathbf{M}_{\text{pre}}$. Second, sparse mask $S$ only masks a tiny fraction of elements in $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$, so the column direction of $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$ are largely intact. Third, low-rank mask $L$ hides $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$ inside one low-dimensional subspace $V_L$, so the column directions of $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$ are intact in the subspace orthogonal to $V_L$. As a result, we can apply a systematic methodology to leverage such leaked information to obtain a surrogate model $\mathbf{M}_{\text{sur}}$ with the same architecture and comparable performance with $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$. At a high level, our attack methodology has three steps: aligning the permutations of multiple observations of $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$; combining the aligned observations to recover the low-rank mask $L$ and locating the positions that has been masked by the sparse mask $S$ to obtain an intermediate model $\mathbf{M}_{\text{init}}$; fine-tuning $\mathbf{M}_{\text{init}}$ on a small labeled dataset (collected by querying $\mathbf{M}_{\text{vic}}$) to remove the remaining sparse mask $S$ and scaling $D$, resulting in $\mathbf{M}_{\text{sur}}$.

## Limitations
The analysis assumes an honest-but-curious adversary who strictly follows the TEE–REE protocol and does not exploit hardware side-channels or transient execution attacks against the TEE itself. We clarify that we do not claim the universal security of $\text{O}_{\text{ext}}$. Instead, we only evaluate $\text{O}_{\text{ext}}$ against the existing Collapse pipeline.

## What practitioners should do
*   Do not assume that a combination of standard primitives (permutation, scaling, additive/low-rank masks) guarantees model extraction resistance.
*   Investigate the algebraic properties of your chosen obfuscation scheme to see if it reduces to the $\text{Oprior}$ canonical form.
*   If employing TSLP, consider obfuscation primitives that disrupt both row- and column-wise invariants, as demonstrated by the novel primitives in $\text{O}_{\text{ext}}$.
*   Be wary of schemes that rely only on masking fractions of elements or simple reordering, as these preserve structural information exploitable by Collapse.

## Verdict
Read this paper if you are implementing or auditing TSLP defenses for on-device LLMs, as it provides a unified, formal framework showing the inherent structural weakness in many current obfuscation techniques.

## Den's Take

The paper correctly identifies that relying on the algebraic closure of simple masking and permutation primitives is a flawed security assumption for TSLP systems. However, the conclusion that these methods are fundamentally weak needs tempering. The Collapse attack appears to be highly effective against the specific composition $\text{Oprior}$ defined, but its success hinges on the adversary strictly adhering to the protocol and only observing the outputs derived from the canonical structure. If a system introduces noise or randomization that breaks the clean algebraic relationships assumed by the attack—for example, by making the observed weight representations non-deterministic across refreshes—the recovery process described by Collapse likely breaks down, even if the underlying primitives are weak individually. This suggests that defense must move beyond just changing primitives to actively disrupting the *observability* of the structural invariants during runtime.
