# Dormant until Deleted: Clean-Label Backdoors Triggered by Machine Unlearning

Compliance with data privacy mandates like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has forced machine learning developers to implement "machine unlearning" interfaces, enabling users to request the deletion of their training records. However, this compliance vector introduces a severe security flaw: the act of unlearning can be weaponized to transition a model's state. This research shows how an attacker can exploit mandatory deletion requests to trigger a highly stealthy, clean-label dormant backdoor in production image classifiers.

Later, they submit a matching deletion request. Cannot directly modify model parameters, training code, or inference-time pipelines. |

| **Budget** | Low. The attacker only controls $4\%$ total of the target dataset ($\rho_p + \rho_c$) and strictly maintains original, clean labels to bypass automated data curation. |

## Background / Problem Setup

Traditional backdoor attacks poison a target dataset so that the backdoor triggers immediately after training. This immediate activation makes them vulnerable to post-training defense screens such as Neural Cleanse or STRIP. While dormant backdoors delay activation until a specific state transition occurs, prior implementations suffer from structural limitations that make them impractical or easily detectable.

The table below contrasts the proposed technique against existing dormant and unlearning-activated backdoor methods:

| Method | Label Constraint | Activation Trigger | Forgetting Set Source | Co-optimization of Trigger & Camouflage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sleeper Agent** (Souri et al. 2022) | Clean-label | Inference trigger only (Active immediately) | N/A (No unlearning component) | No |

## Methodology

The core mechanism of this attack is a **bilevel optimization framework** that coordinates two distinct adversarial generators:
1. A **Trigger Generator ($G_\phi$)** that crafts sample-specific trigger perturbations.
2. A **Camouflage Generator ($M_\psi$)** that produces label-consistent camouflage samples. These samples generate gradients that actively cancel out the backdoor trigger's influence during initial training.

```
       [Attacker Input] 
         /          \
  (Poisoned)     (Camouflage)
  Triggers B_φ   Camouflage C_φ,ψ
         \          /
       [Target Model Training]  ---> [Latent Model f_θl] (Backdoor Dormant, ASR ~12%)
                |
     [Request Unlearning of C_φ,ψ]
                |
       [Unlearned Model f_θu]   ---> [Activated Model] (Backdoor Active, ASR >80%)
```

### 1. Trigger Construction
To prevent high-frequency visual artifacts that are easily flagged by defenses, the Trigger Generator $G_\phi$ projects its perturbations onto the low-frequency domain using the 2D Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) as described in Equation (3):
$$Plf(z) = \text{IDCT}(Mlf \odot \text{DCT}(z))$$

Where $Mlf$ is a low-frequency mask and $\odot$ is the Hadamard product. The final triggered image $B_\phi(x)$ is computed with clipping and Gaussian smoothing $K$ in Equation (4):
$$B_\phi(x) = K(\text{Clip}(x + \eta_G \cdot \delta_G(x)))$$

### 2. Camouflage Generation & Gradient Opposition
The camouflage samples must suppress the backdoor before unlearning, and release this suppression once they are deleted. The authors enforce this via a **Gradient-opposing Loss** (Equation 15). For each camouflage sample $x$, the gradient of the loss on a trigger-contaminated intermediate sample $x^d$ toward the target label $y_t$ is computed as:
$$gbd = \nabla_{\theta_l} L(f_{\theta_l}(x^d), y_t)$$

The training gradient of the camouflage sample $C_{\phi,\psi}(x)$ toward its original class $y$ is:
$$gc = \nabla_{\theta_l} L(f_{\theta_l}(C_{\phi,\psi}(x)), y)$$

The camouflage generator minimizes the cosine similarity of these two gradients:
$$\mathcal{L}^C_{opp} = 1 + cossim(gc, gbd)$$

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}^C_{opp}$ drives $cossim(gc, gbd)$ toward $-1$. This forces the camouflage gradients to actively oppose and cancel the trigger-induced target gradients. When these camouflage samples are unlearned, this cancellation force is removed, releasing the dormant trigger.

### 3. Bilevel Optimization Workflow
Because the attack success depends on both the pre-unlearning (latent) and post-unlearning model states, the authors simulate both training and unlearning in an inner-loop optimization step:

```python
# Conceptual optimization step for Dual-Generator Learning
for round in range(1, R + 1):
    # Step 1: Generate poisoned and camouflage batches
    P_r = [(B_phi(x), y) for (x, y) in P]
    C_r = [(C_phi_psi(x), y) for (x, y) in C]
    D_r = (D - (P + C)) + P_r + C_r
    
    # Step 2: Inner-loop: Simulate victim training
    theta_l = Train(theta_initial, D_r)
    
    # Step 3: Inner-loop: Simulate unlearning of camouflage subset Ur
    U_r = select_subset(C_r)
    theta_u = Funlearn(theta_l, U_r)
    
    # Step 4: Outer-loop: Backpropagate and update generators
    # Update G_phi to maintain dormancy in theta_l and activation in theta_u
    loss_G = lambda_l * L_latent(theta_l) + lambda_a * L_act(theta_u) + lambda_v * L_vis + lambda_c * L_clean
    phi = phi - alpha_G * grad(loss_G, phi)
    
    # Update M_psi to enforce gradient opposition and target suppression
    loss_C = lambda_o * L_opp(theta_l) + lambda_m * L_margin(theta_l) + lambda_cl * L_clean + lambda_r * L_vis
    psi = psi - alpha_C * grad(loss_C, psi)
```

## Limitations & Open Questions

- **Scale of Evaluation**: The evaluations are limited to small-scale vision datasets (CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-10). It is unclear how easily this gradient-opposing alignment scales to large-scale datasets (ImageNet-1K) or multi-modal models where the latent space is significantly larger.
- **Benign Accuracy Degradation**: After unlearning, there is a visible drop in Benign Accuracy. For example, on CIFAR-10 under First-Order unlearning, the benign accuracy drops from 93.35% to 83.24% (a 10.11% absolute drop). While some drop is normal during unlearning, a double-digit decrease in a production model would likely trigger anomaly detection systems.
- **Optimization Overhead**: Bilevel optimization requires simulating both training and unlearning in the inner loop. This is computationally expensive and difficult to scale to very large architectures.
- **Defense Resistance**: Although the authors demonstrate resistance to STRIP (Section G) and fine-pruning (Figure 5), their evaluation assumes standard defensive parameters. An adaptive defender who specifically monitors gradient anomalies during unlearning might still detect the attack.

## What Practitioners Should Do

Machine learning engineers and platform security teams must establish robust verification controls to mitigate this attack vector:

1. **Implement Gradient Alignment Auditing**: Monitor the gradients of requested data deletions. If a coordinated batch of unlearning requests displays strongly aligned gradient directions that oppose standard classification pathways (similar to the objective in Equation 15), flag the batch for manual review.
2. **Apply Rate-Limiting and Provenance Verification on Deletions**: Restrict unlearning requests strictly to authenticated accounts with a verified history of training contribution. Limit the volume of deletion requests per user IP or API endpoint to prevent sybil accounts from suddenly unlearning a coordinated block of camouflage samples ($\rho_c = 3\%$).
3. **Deploy Post-Unlearning Regression Suites**: Do not rely solely on clean validation accuracy (Benign Accuracy) to verify an unlearned model's health. Run a behavioral regression suite using adversarial perturbation engines to check for sudden model state transitions before pushing the unlearned weights to production.
4. **Prune Weak Activation Paths**: Regularly apply fine-pruning techniques on newly unlearned models to eliminate low-activation pathways before they can be exploited by latent backdoors.

## The Takeaway

The intersection of data privacy regulations and adversarial ML reveals that compliance mechanisms like machine unlearning can be subverted into execution triggers for dormant exploits. For security teams, this emphasizes that the "right to be forgotten" cannot be treated as a simple administrative database operation; it must be audited with the same rigor as the initial data ingestion pipeline.

## Den's Take

I believe we are witnessing a structural shift where compliance mechanisms are weaponized as execution vectors. This paper elegantly demonstrates how GDPR-style deletion requests can act as a remote detonator for backdoors. By coordinating a Trigger Generator and a Camouflage Generator to cancel out adversarial gradients during initial training, the authors exploit the exact mathematical assumptions behind modern unlearning algorithms.

However, I am highly skeptical about how this holds up against partition-based retraining. The paper’s strong results—such as the 87.87% post-unlearning ASR on CIFAR-10—rely heavily on the victim using approximate, gradient-based unlearning like First-Order or PUMA. If a pipeline implements partition-based retraining like SISA, the optimization trajectory is completely reset. Shards containing the dormant trigger but lacking the deleted camouflage samples will train from scratch, meaning the highly sensitive, co-optimized gradient-cancellation dynamics will fail to translate to the final aggregated model. 

As I have argued previously, mitigating backdoor updates in complex optimization pipelines requires tracking how adversarial gradients align, a concept that is highly relevant here to expose and block coordinated gradient-canceling pairs before deletion occurs. Practitioners deploying vision pipelines with compliance APIs should treat gradient-based unlearning as an unvetted security risk and stick to hard partition retraining.
